The bouncer with a bad memory

A hands-on tour of Bloom filters, in four short steps.

Your browser wants to warn you before you open a dodgy website. There are millions of them, and the list changes every day.

Downloading the whole list to your phone is too heavy. So early versions of Chrome kept a tiny, blurry sketch of the list instead. The sketch can never say "yes, definitely". It can only say "definitely not" or "hmm, probably".

That blurry sketch is a Bloom filter. It's like a bouncer who forgets faces but remembers a few odd details: "green jacket, tall, laughs loudly". Anyone matching all three gets a second look.

This strip of bulbs is all the memory the bouncer gets. Type a word and hit Add. The word gets scrambled three different ways, and each scramble points at one bulb. Those bulbs turn on.

Then Check a word. If any of its three bulbs is dark, the bouncer is certain: never seen it. If all three glow, it's a "probably".

Here's the catch. Bulbs never turn off, and different words can share bulbs. Fill the strip with strangers, then check a word you never added. Watch the bouncer wave it through anyway.

Play with the sliders: more bulbs means fewer mistakes, and more scrambles per word helps too, until the strip gets crowded.

It's 1970. Burton H. Bloom, a programmer at Computer Usage Company in Massachusetts, publishes a five-page paper in Communications of the ACM: "Space/Time Trade-offs in Hash Coding with Allowable Errors".

The problem

Computers had tiny fast memory (core) and big slow storage (disk). Bloom's example was a program that adds hyphens to words. About 90% of words follow simple rules. The awkward 50,000 or so need a dictionary lookup, and the dictionary only fits on disk.

The naive fix

Look every word up on disk. Slow, because most trips to the disk come back empty-handed.

The insight

Keep a blurry sketch of the awkward words in fast memory. A "definitely not" skips the disk. A "probably" pays for one disk trip, and the occasional false alarm just costs a wasted trip, not a wrong hyphen. Bloom worked out this cut disk visits by about 84%.

Since then

The idea sat quietly for decades, then took off in the 1990s and 2000s when web caches, databases and networks all hit the same wall: too much data, too little fast memory. Cousins appeared too: counting Bloom filters (which can delete), cuckoo filters, and learned filters that use a small model as the first pass.

Notice what Bloom actually traded: he gave up a little correctness to gain a lot of memory. That trade is the whole trick, and it's why the strip above breaks the way it does.

That "definitely not" answer is the whole point. It lets a system skip expensive work with total confidence, and only pay the full price on a "probably".

Browsers and URL checks

Is this site on the danger list? A dark bulb means "no, carry on" with no network call at all.

Databases like Cassandra and RocksDB

Is this key inside this file on disk? Each file can keep a Bloom filter so the database skips files that definitely don't have it.

Caches and CDNs

Has anyone asked for this item before? Only cache it the second time, so one-hit wonders don't clog the shelf.

What it can't do: it never says a definite yes, and you can't remove a word once added, because turning off a bulb might erase someone else's clue.
