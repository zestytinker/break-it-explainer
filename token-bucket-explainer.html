The bouncer with a bag of tickets

A hands-on tour of token-bucket rate limiting, in six short steps.

Your API is popular. One client starts hammering it with thousands of requests a second, and everyone else's requests queue up behind them. You need a way to say slow down that still lets normal users move fast.

The obvious rule, at most N requests per second, punishes honest bursts. A phone app that wakes up and syncs ten things at once gets blocked, while a bot that sends exactly N per second sails through.

Picture a bouncer with a bag of tickets. Every request costs a ticket. Tickets drip into the bag at a steady rate, and the bag has a fixed size. Show up after a quiet spell and you can spend a handful at once. Keep hammering and you wait for the drip.

The bag is the bucket, tickets are tokens. Two numbers describe it: how fast tokens drip in (the rate) and how many the bag holds (the burst). A request takes one token if there is one, or gets refused.

Fire requests one at a time and watch the level drop and refill. Then fire a burst and see the bag empty out.

Here's the catch. A big bucket lets through a big burst, which is exactly the spike you were trying to prevent. A tiny bucket blocks the honest phone app. There is no setting that is right for every client.

The token bucket comes from computer networking, where it was used to control how fast a link could send packets and how big a burst it could absorb.

The problem

Networks in the 1980s needed a way to let a sender burst briefly without letting it flood the link.

The naive fix

Cap the sending rate at a fixed number of packets per second. Fair, but it throws away the burst capacity that real traffic needs.

The insight

Separate the two knobs: an average rate and a burst size. Tokens accumulate while the sender is quiet, so a quiet sender earns the right to burst.

Since then

The idea moved from routers to web APIs.

The trade: you accept short bursts in exchange for a simple, predictable average. The bucket size is the size of the burst you are willing to eat.

Anywhere something has to be fair over time but forgiving in the moment.

Linux traffic control

The kernel's traffic shaping tools use token buckets to limit bandwidth per queue.

API gateways

Reportedly, amazon API Gateway describes its throttling in terms of a rate and a burst, which is a token bucket.

Video streaming

Players are said to use a bucket to smooth out chunks so playback does not stutter.

What it can't do: it limits one client at a time, so it does nothing about total load across all clients, and clocks that drift between servers can let a client double-spend tokens.
